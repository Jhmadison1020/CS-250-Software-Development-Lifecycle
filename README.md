# How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating “user stories” help with this?
  I interpret user needs by actively listening to user feedback. During a user meeting, I will listen to key phrases that indicate a want, desire, or need. Some of these phrases will start with "I would like to see", "I would love", or "It would be better if". These are generic and obvious phrases that indicate a want or need from the user. Other indicators include questions that imply a roadblock or headwind. From this specific project, one of the users asked why destinations would appear in their top 10 list when they never go to destinations like that. This indicates frustration or a roadblock, and to make their life easier when selecting a destination, removing destinations that are never searched or visited will help. To implement these into the program, I would interpret their overall desire and break down the larger ask into smaller, manageable, and meaningful actions that can be converted into software. By doing so, I will have aligned the users' wants and needs with program requirements and defined acceptance criteria. User stories help to highlight the feedback provided. To utilize the users' feedback when creating user stories, I would follow the format "As a..., I want to..., so that...". This helps describe the person who this affects, the action they would like to perform, and the reason this action is so important. This will also aid in selecting priority levels for each task that needs to be completed. 

# How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?
  Although every program I have developed or worked on is different, my approach is generally the same each time. Since the start of this program, each class has expressed the severe importance of incremental work or developing in chunks and not entire programs at once. Each program I am asked to work on starts with a couple of preparatory steps. This includes documenting and understanding program requirements and developing Pseudocode to help guide me in my efforts to write the code. Starting by documenting the requirements helps me fully understand what is being asked. This could be in the form of user stories or a simple list documenting the actions that are planned to take place within the program. Then I will start to break these requirements down into sections of pseudocode. Once one section looks good, I will develop code in relation to that section of the pseudocode, test it, and move on once it satisfies the requirements. During this process, I will use notes and the textbooks I have to work through personal roadblocks when I get stuck. I began this process after one of my first projects because I struggled severely to find all the bugs after writing the entire program in one go. In my future development work, I hope to incorporate two primary Agile processes: the constant user feedback loop and the retrospective. Every process in Agile is important to development, and they are even more powerful when all are utilized together, but I believe these 2 stand out the most. Since the program being developed isn't for myself, I think the user feedback loop is invaluable. This helps my team and me maximize the product value by delivering the desired product instead of our interpretation of what it should be. The retrospective is important because it allows the team of developers to reflect on their work. I have always believed you only fail when you don't learn something. So, even in the event of a missed deadline, a failed product, or an unsatisfied customer, you can always count it as a success if you reflect on the work and learn something that will make you better for future projects. 

# What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?
  There are many attributes that contribute to being a good team member in software development. Some of these attributes include:
   * Working with integrity.
   * Doing your part.
   * Working within your knowledge base.
   * Being accepting of criticism or constructive feedback.
   * Respect.
   * Demonstrating extreme ownership.
  These are just some in a long list, but every attribute aligns with being a good team member because you are collaborating on a product that will impact everyone on the team. True collaboration starts with these attributes because everyone on the team deserves respect. Respect creates a safe and welcoming work environment that boosts morale and promotes efficiency. Extreme ownership is important because it shows your dedication to the team and the product being developed. Criticism and the acceptance of constructive feedback are important because they allow team members to remove arrogance and ego from the team. This will allow individuals to see areas they need to work on and will make them better team members as a whole. Working within your knowledge base and with integrity are crucial to team environments. The people you work with need to be able to trust you, what you say, and the work you are creating. If you don't know something it is your responsibility to be honest and say something. This could lead to a learning process where you seek help in the development of the program or shift those responsibilities to someone who knows what to do, and you can then take on a portion of the product you are more proficient in. This will protect you and your team. Doing your part is important because it is a team effort. If everyone does their part, it spreads out the workload and reduces burnout, which boosts efficiency.
